\title{\bf How Faithful Is the Circuit You Found?\\[2pt]
Post-Selection-Valid Confidence Bounds for Mechanistic Interpretability}

\author{Anonymous}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Circuit papers end with a number: ``our circuit recovers $87\%$ of the model's
performance.'' That number is almost always the largest of many, because the
circuit was chosen by maximising it on the very interventions used to score it.
A maximum over a search is biased upwards, and an error bar computed as though no
search had happened is too narrow: for $100$ equally good hypotheses the nominal
$95\%$ interval covers \MyNaiveCovHundred{} of the time.

We treat graded faithfulness --- interchange-intervention accuracy, logit
difference recovered, behavioural agreement under ablation --- as a population
quantity, treat circuit search as a selection rule, and supply lower confidence
bounds that stay valid however the circuit was chosen. We prove that the
inflation of a selected score is of order $\sigma\sqrt{\log|\Cclass|/n}$ in the
size of the searched class and the number of interventions, and cannot be argued
away. We then give five corrections --- an empirical-Bernstein union bound, a
prior-weighted (Occam) bound that handles all $2^{144}$ subsets of GPT-2 small's
attention heads and makes small circuits cheaper to certify than large ones, an
empirical bootstrap-$t$ simultaneous band that adapts to the strong correlation
between overlapping circuits, sample splitting, and selective intervals for the
winner --- and validate their coverage exactly on four tiny transformers with
known algorithms.

On GPT-2 small's indirect-object-identification circuit, searching
\MyIOIm{} circuit hypotheses with $n=1000$ interventions, the naive interval
covers \MyIOINaiveCov{} of the time; the bootstrap-$t$ band covers
\MyIOIBootCov{} and still certifies faithfulness of \MyIOIBootLCB{}, against a
(inflated) naive point estimate of \MyIOINaivePoint{}. The correction is
therefore cheap --- but only if two other things are done right, and both are
usually not. First, if the target population is new prompt \emph{templates} rather than new
fills of the same ones, the effective sample size shrinks by a factor of
\MyIOIDeff{} and the naive interval's coverage falls to \MyClusterNaiveCov{}
\emph{and keeps falling as more prompts are added}. Second, if the search was adaptive, a bound uniform over the circuits the search
could have reached is valid but vacuous, and interventions must be held out
instead. We release a library
that turns any per-instance faithfulness score into a certificate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A mechanistic interpretability paper typically ends with a sentence of the form
``our circuit recovers $87\%$ of the model's performance''. Behind that sentence
there is a search. The investigator considered many candidate circuits --- which
attention heads to keep, which subspace of a layer's activations to align with
which variable of the hypothesised algorithm, which token positions to intervene
at --- scored each of them on a set of interventions, and reported the best. The
number that reaches the reader is therefore a maximum over a search, not a
measurement of a hypothesis fixed in advance. Maxima of noisy quantities are
biased upwards, and standard errors computed as though no search had happened are
too small. \textbf{This paper supplies the missing warranty: lower confidence
bounds on faithfulness that remain valid no matter how the circuit was chosen,
together with a measurement of what the usual practice costs.} The problem is the
winner's curse, and it is the same one that motivated valid post-selection
inference in statistics \citep{berk2013valid,lee2016exact}, the reusable holdout
in adaptive data analysis \citep{dwork2015reusable}, and the study of leaderboard
overfitting in machine learning
\citep{blum2015ladder,recht2019imagenet,roelofs2019metaanalysis}.

Mechanistic interpretability has good reason to care. \emph{Causal abstraction}
\citep{geiger2025causalabstraction} --- the framework that turns ``this algorithm
describes this network'' into a testable statement, by requiring the two to agree
under matched interventions --- gives the field a precise notion of what a circuit
hypothesis claims, and a graded version of it (how \emph{often} the network's
behaviour under an intervention matches the algorithm's) is what circuit papers
actually report. If that
number is systematically optimistic, then so is the field's picture of how well
it understands its models. And the searches involved are large: automated circuit
discovery sweeps thousands of subgraphs \citep{conmy2023towards,syed2024attribution},
distributed alignment search optimises over rotations of the residual stream
\citep{geiger2024finding}, and a manual analysis of a single behaviour can
compare dozens of hypotheses \citep{wang2023interpretability}.

\paragraph{What this paper does.}
We treat graded faithfulness as a population quantity, treat circuit search as a
selection rule, and ask for confidence bounds that survive the selection.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{We quantify the optimism} (Section~\ref{sec:problem}). For a class
of $m$ hypotheses evaluated on $n$ interventions, the expected inflation of the
reported score is at most $\sigma\sqrt{2\log m/n}$ and, in the worst case, at
least a constant times $\sigma\sqrt{\log m / n}$ when the hypotheses are
independent, so the worst case cannot be improved by better analysis. We also give a closed form
for the coverage of the naive interval, which depends sharply on how correlated
the hypotheses are.

\item \textbf{We give five post-selection-valid constructions} (Section~\ref{sec:methods}):
a distribution-free empirical-Bernstein union bound; a prior-weighted (``Occam'')
version that handles classes as large as all subsets of the 144 attention heads
of GPT-2 small, and under which small circuits are cheaper to certify than large
ones; a bootstrap-$t$ simultaneous band that adapts to the correlation between
overlapping circuits; sample splitting, which is the right answer when the search
is adaptive; and selective (conditional) intervals for the winner, which we find
help only when the winner wins clearly.

\item \textbf{We identify the unit of replication as a first-order issue}
(Section~\ref{sec:cluster}). Interpretability datasets are generated from a small
number of prompt templates. Resampling prompts answers a question about new fills
of the same templates; resampling templates answers a question about the
behaviour itself. The two differ by a \emph{design effect} --- the factor by
which clustering inflates the variance of an average --- which we measure to be
\MyIOIDeff{} on the indirect-object-identification (IOI) task.

\item \textbf{We validate coverage exactly} (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) on
four tiny transformers trained to $100\%$ accuracy on algorithmic tasks --- one
of which has a known-faithful alignment planted by interchange intervention
training --- and then on GPT-2 small's IOI circuit. Because we cache a
per-instance score matrix once and treat the cached pool as the population, the
true faithfulness of every hypothesis is known exactly and measured coverage
carries no oracle noise.

\item \textbf{We give a protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) and a library that
converts any per-instance faithfulness score into a certificate.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why this is not just statistics with a new application.}
Four things about faithfulness evaluation are not generic, and each forced a
change to the standard machinery. \emph{(i)} The dominant scores are binary
(did the intervened network agree with the algorithm?), and for binary scores the
sample mean and sample standard deviation are dependent, which breaks the
textbook multiplier bootstrap; we had to recompute the variance inside every
replicate to recover nominal coverage (Section~\ref{sec:methods}(c)).
\emph{(ii)} The most widely reported metric is a ratio of means, not a mean, so
the finite-sample bounds do not apply to it at all and it needs its own
influence-function band (Section~\ref{sec:ratio}). \emph{(iii)} Circuit classes
are power sets of model components, which is what makes a size-stratified prior
the natural device and yields the interpretability-relevant conclusion that
\emph{minimal circuits are cheaper to certify}. \emph{(iv)} The unit of
replication is a prompt template, not a prompt, and choosing between the two
changes the effective sample size by more than any of the corrections do
(Section~\ref{sec:cluster}). The empirical quantities we report --- how much a
circuit search inflates its own score, how many hypotheses a class is
\emph{effectively} worth, how correlated circuits are --- are measurements about
interpretability practice, not about statistics.

\paragraph{What this paper is not.}
We do not propose a new faithfulness metric, a new circuit-discovery algorithm,
or a new notion of what a circuit is. We take the field's metrics and searches as
given and ask what can honestly be claimed from them. Nor do we claim that
published circuits are wrong: a bound that is valid after selection is usually
only modestly below the naive estimate (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), and our
GPT-2 results certify a genuinely faithful IOI circuit. The claim is that the
guarantee has to be earned, and that earning it is cheap.

\section{Setup: graded faithfulness as a population quantity}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Interventions as data.}
Let $Z$ denote everything random about a single faithfulness measurement. In an
interchange-intervention experiment \citep{geiger2021causalabstractions},
$Z=(b,s)$ is a pair of inputs: a \emph{base} $b$ and a \emph{source} $s$. In an
ablation experiment, $Z$ is a single input. We assume
$Z_1,\dots,Z_n \sim P$ independently; Section~\ref{sec:cluster} relaxes this to
clustered sampling and explains why the relaxation matters.

\paragraph{Hypotheses and scores.}
A \emph{circuit hypothesis} $c$ is any object that, together with $Z$, determines
a bounded per-instance score $\varphi(c;Z)\in[0,1]$. Two instances cover most of
the literature:

\emph{(i) Alignment hypotheses.} $c$ names a location in the network (a
component, a token position) and a linear subspace of the representation there,
and claims that this subspace carries a high-level variable $V$. The score is
the interchange-intervention indicator
\[
\varphi(c;(b,s)) \;=\; \mathbf{1}\Big\{\, f_{\mathrm{low}}\big(b \,;\, \text{subspace} \leftarrow \text{its value on } s\big)
\;=\; f_{\mathrm{high}}\big(b \,;\, V \leftarrow V(s)\big) \Big\},
\]
whose mean is \emph{interchange-intervention accuracy}
\citep{geiger2022inducing,geiger2024finding,wu2023interpretability}.

\emph{(ii) Component-subset hypotheses.} $c$ is a set of model components; every
component outside $c$ is ablated (here: replaced by its mean over a reference
distribution), and $\varphi$ records whether the ablated model still behaves like
the full model \citep{wang2023interpretability,conmy2023towards,chan2022causal}.

We write $\theta(c)=\Ex_P[\varphi(c;Z)]$ for \textbf{graded faithfulness} and
$\thetahat_n(c)=n^{-1}\sum_i \varphi(c;Z_i)$ for its estimate. Section~\ref{sec:ratio}
extends everything to the normalised logit difference recovered, which is a ratio
of means rather than a mean.

\paragraph{Search.}
A \emph{selection rule} is any measurable map $\hchat=A(Z_1,\dots,Z_n)$ into the
hypothesis class $\Cclass$. Nothing below requires $A$ to be the arg-max: it may
be a greedy pruner, a gradient-based alignment search, or an investigator looking
at a plot.

\begin{definition}[Post-selection validity]
A data-dependent function $L(\cdot)$ is a \emph{post-selection-valid} lower
confidence bound at level $\alpha$ if
$\Prb\big(\theta(\hchat)\ge L(\hchat)\big)\ge 1-\alpha$
for \emph{every} selection rule $\hchat$ that is measurable with respect to the
data used to build $L$.
\end{definition}

The cleanest sufficient condition, and the one all our band-type methods satisfy,
is simultaneity: $\Prb(\forall c\in\Cclass:\ \theta(c)\ge L(c))\ge 1-\alpha$.
Simultaneity is strictly stronger than what is needed, but it buys something
useful: it certifies every circuit the investigator looked at, including an
entire reported Pareto frontier, not just the winner.

\section{The problem: how optimistic is a selected faithfulness score?}
\label{sec:problem}

\begin{proposition}[Upper bound on the optimism]
\label{prop:upper}
Let $|\Cclass|=m$, $\sigma^2_{\max}=\max_c \Var\varphi(c;Z)$ and let $\hchat$ be
any selection rule. Then
\[
\Ex\big[\thetahat_n(\hchat)-\theta(\hchat)\big]
\;\le\; \Ex \sup_{c\in\Cclass}\big(\thetahat_n(c)-\theta(c)\big)
\;\le\; \sigma_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{2\log m}{n}} + \frac{\log m}{3n}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[The rate is attained]
\label{prop:lower}
Suppose the centred scores are i.i.d.\ $N(0,\sigma^2)$ across instances and
independent across hypotheses, and $\theta(c)\equiv\theta_0$. Then for
$\hchat=\argmax_c\thetahat_n(c)$,
\[
\Ex\big[\thetahat_n(\hchat)-\theta(\hchat)\big] \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\,\Ex\Big[\max_{j\le m} N_j\Big]
\;\ge\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt 2}\sqrt{\frac{\log m}{n}},\qquad m\ge 3 .
\]
\end{proposition}

Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Together the two propositions pin the
optimism at order $\sigma\sqrt{\log m/n}$: it cannot be argued away by a better
analysis, only reduced by collecting more interventions or searching a smaller
class. The dependence on the class size is mild --- a hundredfold larger search
inflates the optimism by only about $40\%$ when $m\approx 100$, because
$\sqrt{\log m}$ grows slowly --- but the dependence on $n$ is the usual
$n^{-1/2}$, so the optimism does not vanish at any sample size an
interpretability experiment is likely to use. What the propositions do
\emph{not} pin down is the constant in a given study, because that depends on
$\sigma$ and on how correlated the hypotheses are. Correlation is the crucial
study-specific quantity, as the next result shows.

\begin{proposition}[Exact coverage of the naive interval under the complete null]
\label{prop:naivecov}
Suppose $\theta(c)\equiv\theta_0$ and the standardised estimates
$G_c=\sqrt n(\thetahat_n(c)-\theta_0)/\sigma(c)$ are jointly Gaussian with
equicorrelation $\rho$, with $\sigmahat$ treated as known (exact in the Gaussian
limit). The one-sided naive bound
$\thetahat_n(\hchat)-z_{1-\alpha}\sigma(\hchat)/\sqrt n$ at the arg-max covers
if and only if $\max_c G_c \le z_{1-\alpha}$, so its coverage is
\[
\Ex_{W\sim N(0,1)}\Big[\Phi\big((z_{1-\alpha}-\sqrt{\rho}\,W)/\sqrt{1-\rho}\big)^{m}\Big],
\]
which equals $\Phi(z_{1-\alpha})^m$ at $\rho=0$.
\end{proposition}

Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}(a) plots this. At $m=100$ the nominal $95\%$ bound
covers \MyNaiveCovHundred{} of the time when hypotheses are independent and
\MyNaiveCovHundredRhoNine{} when they are $90\%$ correlated. Circuit hypotheses
\emph{are} strongly correlated --- overlapping circuits share most of their
components, so their per-instance scores move together --- which is why the
situation in practice is bad but not catastrophic, and why a method that adapts
to the correlation is worth having.

\section{Bounds that survive the search}
\label{sec:methods}

All five constructions below take the same input: the $n\times m$ matrix
$S_{ic}=\varphi(c;Z_i)$ of per-instance scores. Computing it once is the only
model-forward cost; everything after is arithmetic.

\paragraph{(a) Empirical-Bernstein union bound (\textsc{union-eb}).}
For $X_i\in[0,1]$ i.i.d., \citet{maurer2009empirical} show that with probability
at least $1-\delta$,
$\Ex X \ge \bar X - \sqrt{2 V_n \log(2/\delta)/n} - 7\log(2/\delta)/(3(n-1))$,
where $V_n$ is the sample variance. Taking $\delta=\alpha/m$ and a union bound
gives a simultaneous band. It is distribution-free and finite-sample, so it is
valid for \emph{any} selection rule, and its variance adaptivity matters here:
a faithful circuit scores close to $1$ on almost every instance and so has tiny
variance. Hoeffding's bound is tighter when scores sit near the middle of the
range; running both at $\alpha/2$ and taking the larger bound is valid and
removes the need to guess (\textsc{union-best}).

\paragraph{(b) Prior-weighted (Occam) bound (\textsc{occam}).}
Replace $\alpha/m$ by $\alpha\pi(c)$ for any prior $\pi$ on $\Cclass$ fixed
before seeing data:
\[
\theta(c)\ \ge\ \thetahat_n(c)-\sqrt{\frac{2V_n(c)L_c}{n}}-\frac{7L_c}{3(n-1)},
\qquad L_c=\log\frac{2}{\alpha\pi(c)},
\]
simultaneously over $\Cclass$. With the size-stratified prior over subsets of $N$
components, $\pi(c)=\big[(N{+}1)\binom{N}{|c|}\big]^{-1}$, so
the price a circuit pays grows with $\log\binom{N}{|c|}$. At $N=144$ (the
attention heads of GPT-2 small) that is $34.3$ nats for a $10$-head circuit and
$97.1$ nats for a $72$-head circuit; at $n=2000$ interventions and a score with
standard deviation $0.3$ the two bounds sit $0.10$ and $0.21$ below the point
estimate. \emph{Small circuits are statistically cheaper to certify}, which aligns
the statistics with the field's preference for minimal circuits. This is the only
one of the five that applies when the class is the full power set of
components.

\paragraph{(c) Bootstrap simultaneous band (\textsc{boot-max}).}
Resample the $n$ interventions with replacement, recompute \emph{both} the mean
$\thetahat^{*}_n(c)$ and the standard deviation $\sigmahat^{*}(c)$ of every
hypothesis, and let $\hat q_{1-\alpha}$ be the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of the
studentised maximum
$\max_c \sqrt n\,(\thetahat^{*}_n(c)-\thetahat_n(c))/\sigmahat^{*}(c)$. Then
\[
L(c)\;=\;\thetahat_n(c)-\hat q_{1-\alpha}\,\sigmahat(c)/\sqrt n
\]
is an asymptotically valid simultaneous band even when $m$ grows much faster
than $n$ \citep[Thm.~4.3]{cck2019moment}; the non-studentised counterpart is
\citet[Cor.~3.1]{chernozhukov2013gaussian}, and Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} states
the conditions. Unlike Bonferroni it adapts to the dependence between
hypotheses.

\emph{One failure mode, and its fix.} The band is studentised, so a hypothesis
that scores perfectly on \emph{every sampled} instance has $\sigmahat(c)=0$ and
would be certified at its point estimate --- no resampling of those instances can
discover that its population value is below one. We therefore floor the
studentising standard deviation at $n^{-1/2}$ (in the observed statistic and in
every replicate alike), which gives such a hypothesis an interval of width
$\hat q_{1-\alpha}/n$, the order of the exact ``rule of three''. For classes that
may contain such hypotheses we also provide \textsc{boot-floored}: use the
finite-sample bound of (a) for the degenerate columns and the band for the rest,
each at level $\alpha/2$. Both ingredients are simultaneous over the whole class,
so a union bound over their two failure events makes any data-dependent choice
between them valid at level $\alpha$.

\emph{Recomputing the standard deviation is not optional.} The textbook
multiplier bootstrap holds $\sigmahat(c)$ fixed at its observed value. For the
\emph{binary} scores that dominate interpretability, the sample mean and the
sample standard deviation are strongly dependent --- for a score above $1/2$, an
upward deviation of the mean necessarily comes with a smaller standard deviation
--- so the true studentised maximum has a heavier upper tail than a fixed-$\sigmahat$
bootstrap can reproduce. In our simulations with $m=60$ hypotheses and $n=400$
interventions the fixed-$\sigmahat$ band covers \MyFixedSigmaCov{} instead of
$95\%$, while the version above covers \MyBootTCov{}. We therefore use the
empirical (resampling) bootstrap-$t$ throughout, and the same correction applies
to the cluster-robust version of Section~\ref{sec:cluster}. We summarise the
adaptivity to dependence by the

\begin{definition}[Multiplicity factor and effective number of hypotheses]
Let $\hat q^{\,\mathrm{marg}}_{1-\alpha}$ be the critical value the \emph{same}
bootstrap gives for a single, pre-specified hypothesis. The \emph{multiplicity
factor} is $\kappa=\hat q_{1-\alpha}/\hat q^{\,\mathrm{marg}}_{1-\alpha}$ --- the
factor by which searching widens the interval --- and the \emph{effective number
of hypotheses} is $m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\alpha/\big(1-\Phi(\kappa\,z_{1-\alpha})\big)$,
the number of independent hypotheses a Bonferroni correction would have to cover
to pay the same price.
\end{definition}

Dividing by the marginal critical value matters: $\hat q_{1-\alpha}$ also absorbs
the finite-sample non-normality of the studentised statistic, which has nothing
to do with searching. The natural comparison for $\kappa$ is the Bonferroni
factor $z_{1-\alpha/m}/z_{1-\alpha}$ that a correction over $m$ \emph{independent}
hypotheses would pay. In our simulations (Table~\ref{tab:synth}) the two are
close when hypotheses are independent and $\kappa$ falls well below Bonferroni
once they are correlated --- at $|\Cclass|=2000$ and correlation $0.9$,
$\kappa=1.99$ against a Bonferroni factor of $2.47$. $\kappa$ can also
\emph{exceed} the Bonferroni factor, and does so on IOI, where many circuits are
perfect on every sampled instance and the studentised maximum is correspondingly
heavy-tailed; that is a finite-sample effect, not multiplicity, and it is the
situation the floored variant of paragraph~(c) is for.

\paragraph{(d) Sample splitting (\textsc{split}).}
Select on a fraction $f$ of the instances and bound on the rest. Valid for
arbitrary and \emph{adaptive} selection, and free of any dependence on
$|\Cclass|$. We report two versions: \textsc{split} uses a $t$-interval on the
held-out half, which is the like-for-like comparison with the (also asymptotic)
bootstrap band, and \textsc{split-eb} uses the finite-sample bound of (a) at
$m=1$. Comparing half-widths of the two asymptotic procedures, the band is narrower than
a $50/50$ split exactly when $\kappa\le\sqrt 2$, i.e.\ when
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}\lesssim 5$ at $\alpha=0.05$. That comparison is not the whole
story, because splitting also degrades the \emph{selection}: $\hchat$ is chosen
from half the instances, so its true faithfulness is lower. The net effect has to
be measured, and it goes the band's way whenever no hypothesis is clearly best
and splitting's way when one is (Table~\ref{tab:synth}).

\paragraph{(e) Selective intervals for the winner (\textsc{cond}, \textsc{hybrid}).}
Conditioning on the event $\{\argmax=\hchat\}$ makes $\thetahat_n(\hchat)$ a
truncated normal \citep{lee2016exact,andrews2024inference}, which can be inverted
for a conditionally valid bound. We implement it, and report that it is
informative only when the winner wins clearly; with several near-tied hypotheses
--- the normal situation in circuit search --- the truncation point approaches
the observed value and the interval degenerates. The hybrid construction of \citet{andrews2024inference} repairs this; ours is a
conservative variant of it (we intersect with a two-sided simultaneous band
rather than the exact projection region, so the level is at least $1-\alpha$ and
in practice somewhat above). We include both for completeness and recommend
\textsc{boot-max} as the default.

\begin{remark}[Randomised selection is not a free lunch]
For a Gaussian mean vector one can select on $U=\thetahat+\tau\xi$ and infer from
$V=\thetahat-\tau^{-1}\hat\Sigma\xi/n$, which are independent
\citep{leiner2025fission,garciarasines2023splitting}. Setting
$\tau^2=a\sigma^2/n$ gives $\Var(U)=\sigma^2(1+a)/n$ and
$\Var(V)=\sigma^2(1+1/a)/n$ --- exactly the variances of an $f=1/(1+a)$ sample
split. Randomisation buys a continuous knob, not extra information; recovering
information requires data carving \citep{fithian2014optimal}, which we leave to
future work.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Metrics that are not means}
\label{sec:ratio}

The most widely reported faithfulness metric is the \emph{normalised logit
difference recovered},
\[
F(c)=\frac{\Ex[\mathrm{LD}(c)]-\Ex[\mathrm{LD}(\emptyset)]}{\Ex[\mathrm{LD}(\text{full})]-\Ex[\mathrm{LD}(\emptyset)]},
\]
a smooth functional of a mean vector rather than a mean. Its influence function
is given in Appendix~\ref{app:ratio}; feeding it to the same bootstrap-$t$
gives a simultaneous band for the whole family. The finite-sample bounds (a)--(b)
do not apply directly because $F$ is not an average of bounded quantities; we
give a conservative Fieller-style alternative in the appendix.

\subsection{Adaptive search}
\label{sec:adaptive}

A greedy pruner issues thousands of queries, each depending on the previous
answers, so a union bound over the queries \emph{actually asked} is not valid ---
the query set is itself random. Three options remain.
\textbf{(i) Split.} Search on one part, certify on a held-out part. In
interpretability the evaluation data is usually synthetic and nearly free, which
makes this the default recommendation.
\textbf{(ii) Uniformity over the reachable set.} A union or Occam bound over
every circuit the search could have returned. For greedy removal of at most $d$
of $N$ components, $\log|\text{reachable}|=\log\sum_{k\le d}\binom{N}{k}$, which
is $99.8$ nats for $N=144,d=124$ --- a Hoeffding penalty of $0.158$ at $n=2000$.
\textbf{(iii) Reusable holdout.} Thresholdout \citep{dwork2015reusable} answers
adaptive queries from a holdout through a noisy threshold, spending budget only
on queries that overfit. Its theoretical constants are loose, so we report it as
an empirically validated option rather than a certificate.

\section{What population? Clustered interventions}
\label{sec:cluster}

Interpretability datasets are generated from templates: the IOI dataset is
$15$ sentence frames in two name orders, filled with random names, objects and
places. Resampling \emph{prompts} and resampling \emph{templates} answer different
questions:
\[
\theta_{\text{fix}}:\ \text{new fills of these templates},
\qquad
\theta_{\text{new}}:\ \text{new templates as well}.
\]
With $G$ templates, $n/G$ prompts each and intra-template correlation $\rho_I$,
the variance of $\thetahat$ under template-level sampling is inflated by Kish's
design effect $\mathrm{DEFF}=1+(n/G-1)\rho_I$ \citep{kish1965survey}, so the effective sample size is
$n/\mathrm{DEFF}$ and saturates at $G/\rho_I$ however many prompts are generated.
For the simultaneous band we replace the i.i.d.\ resampling by the wild
cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008bootstrap,field2007bootstrapping} with
Rademacher multipliers at the template level, studentised by the cluster-robust
standard error, and inflate the critical value by $t_{G-1}/z$ when $G$ is small
\citep{cameron2015practitioner}. With $G$ clusters the Rademacher weights admit
only $2^G$ distinct sign assignments, so the attainable critical values are
discrete at small $G$ \citep{webb2023reworking,mnw2023clusterrobust}.
Neither estimand is the ``right'' one --- but a paper should say which it means,
and we recommend reporting both.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Design that removes oracle noise.}
For every setting we compute a per-instance score matrix once over a large pool
of instances, then \emph{treat the pool as the population}: analysis samples of
size $n$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from it. The true faithfulness $\theta(c)$ of every
hypothesis is then the pooled column mean, known exactly. A measured coverage
is therefore a Monte-Carlo average of an event whose truth value we know, and
its only error is Monte-Carlo error over replications (with $R=400$ replications
the standard error near $0.95$ is $0.011$); it is not contaminated by
uncertainty about the truth, which is the usual obstacle to measuring coverage on
a real model. All bounds are one-sided at $\alpha=0.05$; coverage means
$\Prb(L(\hchat)\le\theta(\hchat))$, and among procedures that attain it we rank
by the \emph{certified value} $L(\hchat)$ --- higher is better.

\subsection{E1: controlled simulations}
\label{sec:e1}

Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy} isolates the phenomenon in a setting where we control
the class size $m$, the number of interventions $n$, and the correlation $\rho$
between hypotheses, and where every hypothesis has the same true faithfulness so
that any apparent winner is pure selection noise. Panel~(a) confirms
Proposition~\ref{prop:naivecov} numerically and shows that the exact Gaussian
expression tracks simulated binary (interchange-accuracy-like) scores closely.
Panel~(b) confirms the $\sqrt{2\log m/n}$ law of Propositions~\ref{prop:upper}
and~\ref{prop:lower}. Panel~(c), and Table~\ref{tab:synth} in the appendix, answer
the practical question: all of the corrected methods hold their nominal level,
and the ranking among them by \emph{certified value} is governed by
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}$. When hypotheses are strongly correlated the bootstrap band is
the clear winner; when the class is large and the hypotheses are independent,
sample splitting overtakes it. The conditional interval is valid throughout but
collapses to an uninformative bound whenever the winner does not win clearly,
which under the complete null is almost always.

\subsection{E2: tiny transformers with known algorithms}
\label{sec:e2}

We train four small transformers to $100\%$ accuracy on tasks whose high-level
causal model is known by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:details}), and search
over a pre-registered class of alignment or circuit hypotheses. The four settings
were chosen to span the regimes that matter.

\emph{(a) Hierarchical equality, searched (TT-A).} A two-layer transformer
computing $\mathbf{1}\{(a{=}b)=(c{=}d)\}$. Searching \Myttam{} alignment
hypotheses for the variable $V_1=\mathbf{1}\{a{=}b\}$ finds a clear winner: the
whole residual stream at the position of token $b$ after layer $0$, with
interchange accuracy \MyttaThetaBest{}. This is the benign regime --- one
hypothesis is genuinely much better than the rest, and the correction costs
almost nothing.

\emph{(b) The same search restricted to localised subspaces (TT-A-local).} An
investigator who wants a \emph{low-dimensional} representation pre-registers only
the subspaces of at most 16 dimensions. That class has no dominant member, and
its top hypotheses are nearly tied: the near-tie regime, where selection bites.

\emph{(c) Three-term modular arithmetic (TT-C).} A three-layer transformer
computing $(a{+}b{+}c) \bmod 10$. Searching \Myttcm{} alignments for the
intermediate sum $S_1=(a{+}b)\bmod 10$ finds \emph{nothing}: the best population
interchange accuracy is \MyttcThetaBest{} against a chance level of $0.10$. The class is not
at fault: training the same architecture with interchange intervention training
so that $S_1$ \emph{is} carried by a 32-dimensional subspace after layer $1$
produces a model in which that very hypothesis --- a member of the same
pre-registered class --- attains interchange accuracy \MyttciitTheta{}
(Appendix~\ref{app:details}). So the class can express a faithful alignment when
one exists; the ordinarily trained network simply does not have one at any single
position. This is the regime that matters most, because it is where a naive
report is most dangerous: at $n=1000$ the best-scoring hypothesis is reported as
\MyttcNaivePoint{} on average, which an unwary reader would take as evidence of
partial localisation.

\emph{(d) Induction circuits (TT-B).} An attention-only two-layer model on the
induction task, with the hypothesis class the full power set of its $8$ heads.
Here the \emph{unconstrained} arg-max is uninteresting --- \MyttbPerfect{} of the
256 subsets reproduce the full model's predictions on every instance --- so we
report the selection an investigator would actually make, the best circuit of at
most two heads, whose true faithfulness is \MyttbThetaBest{}. This setting also
exercises the Occam bound with the size-stratified prior.

Table~\ref{tab:tiny} and Figure~\ref{fig:tiny} report the coverage. The pattern
matches the theory: naive coverage degrades exactly where the class contains
near-ties, and every corrected bound holds its level everywhere.

\paragraph{Does the correction cost us the right answer?}
A valid bound is worthless if it makes the search useless. We check this on a
fifth model, TT-A-IIT: the same equality architecture, trained by interchange
intervention training \citep{geiger2022inducing} so that a specific
$16$-dimensional subspace after layer $0$ \emph{is} a faithful carrier of $V_1$
by construction. The planted hypothesis attains population interchange accuracy
\MyPlantedTheta{}, and the search returns a hypothesis with that same accuracy
in \MyPlantedPower{} of replications at $n=1000$. Selection is not the problem;
reporting the selected score without a correction is.

\subsection{E3: the IOI circuit in GPT-2 small}
\label{sec:e3}

We reproduce the standard indirect-object-identification evaluation
\citep{wang2023interpretability}: \MyIOIN{} prompts from \MyIOINTemplates{}
templates, on which GPT-2 small ranks the indirect object above the subject
\MyIOIModelAcc{} of the time with a mean logit difference of \MyIOILDfull{}.
Every attention head outside a circuit is replaced by its average activation over
the ``ABC'' distribution --- the same prompts with three distinct names, so that
name information is removed while sentence structure is preserved --- computed
separately for each template and each token position. This node-level scheme is
the one used by automated circuit discovery \citep{conmy2023towards};
\citet{wang2023interpretability} additionally restrict each circuit head to a
single token position, so the two schemes are not directly comparable and neither
dominates. Under ours the published 26-head circuit recovers \MyIOIPubRatio{} of
the model's logit difference, close to the $87\%$ they report under theirs. The
statistical machinery does not care which scheme is used --- it consumes whatever
per-instance score the investigator defines --- but the reported number does, and
a paper should say which one it means.

\input{generated/tab_main}

Our hypothesis class contains \MyIOIm{} circuits, built without looking at the
data from the seven functional head groups of \citet{wang2023interpretability}
and from size-stratified random subsets. Table~\ref{tab:main} is the headline
result. At $n=1000$ interventions the naive interval covers \MyIOINaiveCov{} of
the time; the bootstrap band covers \MyIOIBootCov{} and gives up only
\MyIOIBootPenalty{} of faithfulness relative to the (inflated) point estimate.
The per-instance scores of different circuits are correlated (median pairwise
correlation \MyIOIMedCorr{}), and searching inflates the interval by a factor of
$\kappa=\MyIOIKappa{}$ against a Bonferroni factor of \MyIOIBonfRatio{} for
\MyIOIm{} independent hypotheses. Here $\kappa$ is the larger of the two, because
a large fraction of the class is perfect on every sampled instance: the price
being paid is not multiplicity but the heavy tail of a studentised maximum over
near-degenerate columns, which is exactly what the floored variant addresses.

\paragraph{Certifying a whole frontier.}
Interpretability papers rarely report one circuit; they report a curve of
faithfulness against circuit size and invite the reader to pick a point on it.
Every point on that curve is a separate selection. Because our band is
simultaneous, it certifies all of them at once: Figure~\ref{fig:ioi}(b) shows
that the entire certified frontier is valid with probability
\MyFrontierCoverage{}, whereas the naive curve exceeds the truth at
\MyFrontierNaiveViolations{} of its points.

\paragraph{The unit of replication.}
Table~\ref{tab:cluster} makes the second practical point. When prompts are drawn
template-by-template --- which is how an IOI evaluation set is actually built ---
the intra-template correlation of per-instance scores is \MyIOIICC{}, giving a
design effect of \MyIOIDeff{} at $n=2000$: those $2000$ prompts carry about as
much information as \MyIOIEffN{} independent ones. The naive interval then covers
\MyClusterNaiveCov{} of the time, and --- the diagnostic point --- its coverage
\emph{falls} as $n$ grows (\MyClusterNaiveCovLow{} at $n=500$), because more
prompts from the same templates sharpen the search without sharpening the
estimate. The i.i.d.\ band degrades the same way, from
\MyClusterBootCovLow{} at $n=500$ to \MyClusterBootCov{} at $n=2000$; the cluster
bootstrap holds its level throughout (\MyClusterClusterCov{}). The effective
sample size saturates at $G/\rho_I$, so the fix is to write more templates, not
to generate more prompts.

\paragraph{Pre-registration is the cheapest fix.}
The published IOI circuit was specified before we generated any data, so a single
marginal interval is valid for it \emph{with respect to our search}. At $n=1000$
that interval certifies \MyPreRegLCB{} --- \MyPreRegGain{} more than what the
same data can certify for a circuit chosen by searching our class. Fixing the
hypothesis first and then generating fresh interventions removes the multiplicity
penalty entirely. Two honest caveats. The circuit is only pre-registered relative
to \emph{us}: it was itself the product of a search by
\citet{wang2023interpretability} over the same model and behaviour, and our
interval does not correct for that search. And our hypothesis class is built
around their circuit, so the comparison measures the cost of our search on top of
theirs, not the cost of search in general.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Causal abstraction and circuit faithfulness.}
\citet{geiger2025causalabstraction} give the theoretical foundation we build on,
unifying activation patching, causal scrubbing \citep{chan2022causal},
distributed alignment search \citep{geiger2024finding} and related methods; the
graded notion of faithfulness we bound is theirs. Circuit analyses
\citep{wang2023interpretability,hanna2023greaterthan,lieberum2023circuit,
marks2025sparsefeaturecircuits} and automated discovery methods
\citep{conmy2023towards,syed2024attribution,bhaskar2024edgepruning,kramar2024atp}
supply the searches whose selection effects we quantify.
\citet{zhang2024bestpractices} and \citet{heimersheim2024activationpatching} give
methodological guidance on activation patching, and \citet{miller2024faithfulness}
show that faithfulness metrics are sensitive to ablation choices; both are
complementary to our question, which is about the sampling and selection
uncertainty of whatever metric is chosen. \citet{makelov2024subspace} exhibit
interpretability illusions in subspace patching --- a hypothesis can score well
for the wrong reason --- which is a validity concern orthogonal to, and not
addressed by, confidence bounds.

\paragraph{Statistical testing in interpretability.}
Closest to us is \citet{shi2024hypothesis}, who test a \emph{given} circuit
hypothesis against nulls built from random or shuffled circuits. Their tests
assume the circuit was fixed in advance; our contribution is precisely the
selection step that precedes such a test. \citet{adolfi2025complexity} show that
circuit discovery is computationally intractable in the worst case, a
complementary negative result about search rather than inference.

\paragraph{Post-selection and adaptive inference.}
Valid post-selection inference \citep{berk2013valid}, exact selective inference
after model selection \citep{lee2016exact,fithian2014optimal,tibshirani2016exact,
tian2018selective}, inference on winners \citep{andrews2024inference},
post-selection inference via algorithmic stability \citep{zrnic2023post} and
splitting strategies \citep{garciarasines2023splitting,leiner2025fission} form the
statistical backbone. Adaptive data analysis
\citep{dwork2015preserving,dwork2015reusable,blum2015ladder,bassily2016algorithmic,
russo2016controlling,feldman2017generalization} provides the tools for sequential
search. Our simultaneous bands rest on high-dimensional Gaussian approximation
\citep{chernozhukov2013gaussian,chernozhukov2017central,chernozhukov2016empirical}
and on empirical-Bernstein concentration \citep{maurer2009empirical,audibert2009exploration}.
The phenomenon is familiar elsewhere: the winner's curse in genetics
\citep{zollner2007winners}, adaptive overfitting on benchmarks
\citep{recht2019imagenet,roelofs2019metaanalysis}, and researcher degrees of
freedom in the empirical sciences \citep{simmons2011false,gelman2013garden,ioannidis2005why}.
To our knowledge none of this machinery has been brought to bear on mechanistic
faithfulness, and the specific structure of the problem --- bounded per-instance
scores, strongly overlapping hypotheses, template-clustered interventions, and
metrics that are ratios of means --- requires the specific combination we assemble.

\section{Conclusion}

Causal abstraction made circuit hypotheses falsifiable. Falsifiability is a
property of a hypothesis fixed in advance; a circuit that was chosen by
maximising the same score it is then evaluated on has not been tested in that
sense. The correction is well understood in statistics, it is cheap in
interpretability's usual regime, and it converts a point estimate into a claim a
reader can rely on. We have supplied the estimator, the guarantees, the
validation, and the code.
